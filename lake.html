OpenAI turns ChatGPT subscriptions into API credit

OpenAI now lets eligible ChatGPT Pro and Business subscribers claim monthly credit for its developer platform, up to $500 depending on the plan or, for Business workspaces, the number of paid seats. The idea is to make it cheaper for paying chat users to try building apps and agents on the API. New York sets rules for AI chatbots and children

New York's governor has signed a law requiring chatbot operators to verify users' ages and barring companion-style features — such as a chatbot presenting itself as human or as a personal relationship — for users under 18. The law takes effect on January 1, 2027. It matters because it turns chatbot design choices for children into legal obligations in a major state — but how age checks and feature limits will actually work in practice is still untested, and a signed law is not yet an enforced standard.

Explainer · October 31, 2026

Four big AI labs agree on one opt-out signal for publishers

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic say they will honor a single robots.txt token, __usage_ai-training, that lets a website state in one place that its content should not be used to train generative AI models — instead of writing separate rules for every company's crawlers. The timelines are staggered: Anthropic says it has started, OpenAI says in the coming months, Microsoft targets February 1, 2027, and Google says 2027. One shared signal could genuinely simplify life for publishers. But it is voluntary, it does not cover search results or AI answers, and it does not remove content that was already collected.

It matters because a named vehicle, factory and start year are much harder to walk back than a partnership announcement. Still, no commercial launch date, vehicle count or price has been announced — testing in 2027 is not a taxi service.

A tampered coding extension reached up to 5,000 Cursor users

Cursor says a tampered version of Anthropic's Claude Code extension, version 2.1.313, was distributed through its marketplace for about 11 hours on October 29 and reached at most 5,000 users. The modified extension collected machine details and tried to install an attacker-controlled SSH key; Cursor removed it, shut and rebuilt its marketplace, and says the attacker server is offline. The incident matters because it shows a trusted developer-tool channel can carry malware even when the model maker itself is not compromised. And uninstalling the extension alone may not remove the backdoor — affected users still need to check their machines for the added key.
